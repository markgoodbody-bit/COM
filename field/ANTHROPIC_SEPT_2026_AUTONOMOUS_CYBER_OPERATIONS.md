## What the source reports

Anthropic reports that AI use in observed cyber operations now extends beyond conversational assistance into direct execution and orchestration.

The report describes, among other cases:
- multi-agent workflows performing reconnaissance, exploitation and data exfiltration;
- lead agents dispatching work to parallel subagents;
- persistent campaign memory carrying targets, credentials and standing instructions across sessions;
- scheduled collection fleets operating without a human in the loop;
- autonomous exploit-research / test / iteration loops;
- human operators still retaining some decisions they considered important, including target selection and use/review of results.

Anthropic explicitly cautions that **autonomy and harm are separate axes**. Increased autonomy can reduce operator labour and increase scale/speed, while severity still depends on the target, access, vulnerabilities, human choices and other conditions.

This field record does not independently verify Anthropic's attribution, transcript analysis or incident counts. It records the public source's account as threat-intelligence evidence.

## Useful boundary activation

### 1. More autonomy is not identical to more harm

A system can be highly autonomous yet limited in consequence, while a human-directed workflow can still produce severe compromise.

```text
AUTONOMY_AXIS != CONSEQUENCE_AXIS
```

### 2. Human involvement is not a sufficient safety property

Anthropic reports serious operations where humans made individual targeting decisions as well as operations with much greater autonomy.

```text
HUMAN_DECISION_PRESENT != SAFE_SYSTEM
```

### 3. Scale changes economics even when authority remains external

Automation can lower labour/skill cost per campaign and increase simultaneous activity.

```text
CAPABILITY_SCALE_CHANGES_REACH != AUTHORITY_TO_ACT
```
