# What Comes Next: The Capability Nobody Could Count Yet

**Optional human-readable interlude — exploratory candidate only.** Not part of the private Mechanical Ethics 1.0 DEV4R CORR2 manuscript, not released, not a claim that the existing v0.8 Saturday Workshop story is deficient or may be overwritten. Written for Mark's optional literary consideration after the human editorial hold. All scenes are constructed.

A workshop opens on Saturday morning. Somebody has repaired the tools, somebody has paid the electricity bill and somebody is there to show a newcomer how to loosen a stubborn bolt without damaging the frame.

From outside, the workshop looks much as it did last week. The same shutters rise, the same benches are occupied, and the same chipped kettle begins to boil.

But something is changing.

Last month, a young visitor came in because a bicycle wheel kept rubbing against the frame. An experienced repairer helped her find the fault. She watched, tried, made a mistake and tried again. Today she has brought a neighbour who wants to learn the same thing. The experienced repairer is busy at the other end of the room. She begins with the part she now understands.

A count of visitors might show no growth. A count of available tools might show no change. But the workshop has acquired a different capability. Someone who needed help can now help somebody else.

We should be careful with that claim. Perhaps she has only memorised one repair. Perhaps the next bicycle has a fault beyond her experience. We will learn more if she can recognise what she doesn't know, ask for help and deal safely with a different wheel. Still, her first independent attempt is evidence of a change that would be invisible in the simplest attendance report.

This is **formation**: what people become able to do through practice, feedback and the conditions other people help sustain.

Now imagine two workshops that submit the same number to a funder: **one trained person**.

In the first, the person with the skill has the time, patience and resources to teach. In the second, the person with the skill is leaving and there is nobody available to continue the teaching. The count is true in both places. It does not answer whether either workshop can pass the skill onwards.

The problem is not that numbers are dishonest. We might be asking them to answer a question they were never designed to answer.

If the question is how many people possess a skill today, a count may be sufficient. If the question is whether the workshop can teach another group next month, the relationships, roles, available hours, tools and places begin to matter. A description can be accurate at one scale and inadequate for a different decision.

This is one reason we have to move between scales rather than choose one perspective and declare it complete.

There is another difficulty.

The workshop might purchase a machine that completes the repair much faster than anyone can do it by hand. Customers benefit. More bicycles return to the road. The repairers spend less time on repetitive work and may have more energy for difficult problems.

Or the same machine might quietly remove the beginner's chance to practise.

Which future follows?

We cannot decide that from the machine's presence alone. We need to know how the workshop uses it. Does somebody still have a chance to learn? Are experienced people available to teach? Is the equipment safe? Who controls it? What happens when it breaks? Who benefits from the time saved? Who loses the income or experience they previously relied on?

The machine may be neither a villain nor a saviour. Its effects pass through arrangements made by people and institutions, alongside physical constraints.

And even if we record what happened last month perfectly, we may still not know what would have happened if the workshop had made a different choice. Perhaps demand would have risen anyway. Perhaps an experienced repairer would have left. Perhaps the machine prevented a closure rather than caused one.

**A changed number is not, by itself, an explanation of what caused the change.**

We need evidence capable of distinguishing the plausible causes. Sometimes a specialist already has the right method. Mechanical Ethics should lead the question towards that method, not pretend that its own vocabulary supplies the missing facts.

There is a final possibility worth noticing.

The new visitor might eventually invent a simpler tool, or discover a repair that makes an old piece of equipment usable again. Someone else may adapt it for a purpose nobody in the workshop anticipated. A practice might develop into a small community business. A friendship may form. A person who first came for a bicycle may discover an interest in engineering.

We can identify some of the conditions that make those things easier or harder. We cannot honestly claim to have listed every future they might create.

In that sense, building a better future is not only selecting from a menu of possibilities already visible to us. Sometimes it means **helping new possibilities become real**.

This does not remove the difficult questions about cost, power and consent.

Somebody must keep the lights on. Tools need repairing. Volunteers need rest. A donor may offer money but demand private information that participants have good reason not to share. A larger operation might help more people while placing new burdens on those least able to refuse.

There may be no painless option.

A useful account should show the available choices, the people and resources needed to sustain them, the benefits that have actually occurred, and the costs that somebody must bear. Where interests conflict, legitimate decisions and relevant expertise remain necessary. A description cannot decide what another person's life ought to mean.

The promise of the workshop is not that every visitor will become a repairer, or that every risk can be eliminated.

It is that people may leave with more than a repaired bicycle: a skill, a relationship, an idea, a route to something else.

And that they should remain free to decide what to do with those possibilities.

When the workshop closes, the benches are empty again. The machinery is silent. The report can count the repairs and the hours.

The account may be complete for the funder's question.

But if we want to understand what the workshop has made possible, we should also look at what its visitors can now do **when the shutter is down**.
