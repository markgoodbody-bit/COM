# COASTAL RETREAT / TRANSFORMATION TRANSFER — CASE F — 2026-09-01

Status: WORKING RESEARCH RETURN / NOT CANON / NOT TRACE OR ME CHANGE / NOT LOCAL PLANNING OR LEGAL ADVICE

Scene: a coastal town has repeatedly repaired a sea wall protecting 300 homes. Rising maintenance costs, erosion and sea-level projections indicate that maintaining the current line for another 40 years would require increasingly large public expenditure and may worsen erosion downstream. A managed-retreat proposal would buy some homes, build new housing inland, restore salt marsh, relocate utilities and phase withdrawal over 15 years. Residents disagree sharply; some families have lived there for generations. Staying, retreating and delaying all create irreversible losses.

Guiding question:

> Can the scaffold reason about deliberate ending/transformation without treating persistence of the current state as the default good?

## 1. Nearest external owners

### Dynamic Adaptive Policy Pathways

DAPP is already a strong owner of planning under deep uncertainty: commit to near-term actions, monitor signposts/triggers, sequence pathways over time, preserve contingency options and avoid lock-in. Coastal guidance using this family of methods explicitly aims to enable future adjustment before damage and to use community values plus monitoring/decision triggers.

Sources:
- https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S095937801200146X
- https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1462901117306068

### Coastal adaptation / managed retreat

Managed retreat is difficult because it requires long lead times, deep restructuring, funding, property/legal arrangements, social acceptance and multi-scale governance. Recent pathway work highlights timing dependencies, lock-in and spatial trade-offs. Political-risk analysis emphasizes distributional impacts as a major implementation barrier.

Sources:
- https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1462901119314303
- https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0964569125004855
- https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0964569116301119

### Well-being / place / transformative adaptation

Coastal adaptation and relocation affect quality of life, mental health, place attachment and community change; managed retreat is not merely an engineering/economic relocation problem.

Source:
- https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev-publhealth-090419-102302

### Territorial / relocation justice

Relocation can impose non-monetary territorial/cultural losses and can also burden host communities. Justice questions include procedure, equal status, cultural/political continuity and the conditions under which people can resettle together rather than only receive individual mobility/compensation.

Source:
- https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-political-science-review/article/climate-displacement-and-territorial-justice/EEB7EF21691F39C8571E946CCBCFE978

## 2. Boundary

Material scopes:
- current households/residents;
- families with long place histories;
- local community institutions/social networks;
- future residents/children;
- downstream coastal communities affected by erosion transfer;
- taxpayers/public funders;
- utilities/infrastructure operators;
- inland/host community receiving relocation/new housing;
- salt-marsh/coastal ecosystem.

Important alternative boundaries:

`TOWN AS CURRENT LOCATION / BUILT FORM`
versus
`COMMUNITY AS RELATIONSHIPS / INSTITUTIONS / PEOPLE`

These are not identical.

`PRESERVING COMMUNITY != PRESERVING EVERY CURRENT PHYSICAL ARRANGEMENT`

But neither does relocation automatically preserve community.

## 3. Action field

Nominal options:
- maintain/raise sea wall;
- managed retreat;
- hybrid/partial protection + retreat;
- delay.

Practical affordances depend on:
- finance/buyout authority;
- availability of inland housing/land;
- utility relocation lead times;
- property/legal process;
- resident/host-community cooperation;
- transport/jobs/schools/social infrastructure;
- ecological restoration capability;
- insurance/market changes;
- time before hazard/maintenance thresholds.

`RETREAT PLAN EXISTS != COMMUNITY CAN ACTUALLY TRANSITION`

Delay also changes the action field: property values, insurance, infrastructure failure and hazard can make later retreat more expensive or less humane.

`DELAY IS PATH CONSTRUCTION`.

## 4. Viability type check

Viability is useful only with explicit object/constraints.

Possible objects:
- viability of holding the current shoreline;
- viability of public expenditure trajectory;
- viability of household occupation under flood/erosion risk;
- viability of community continuity after relocation;
- ecological viability of downstream/salt-marsh systems.

These are not one objective.

The current settlement line can become non-viable under selected cost/risk/ecological constraints while the community remains capable of transformation.

`CURRENT FORM VIABILITY != COMMUNITY VIABILITY`

## 5. Capability / transition capability

Managed retreat is a capability-building problem before it is a relocation event.

Necessary capacities may include:
- funding/buyout mechanism;
- replacement housing;
- land and planning consent;
- utility/infrastructure relocation;
- legal/property process;
- social/community planning;
- host-community capacity;
- transport/jobs/services;
- salt-marsh restoration capability;
- evidence/monitoring triggers;
- support for residents who cannot navigate relocation unaided.

This strongly confirms:

`SYSTEM PATHWAY EXISTS != AFFECTED ENTITY CAN TRANSITION`

and:

`FAILURE THRESHOLD != LATEST SAFE DECISION TIME`

If replacement housing/utilities take a decade, waiting until the wall is near failure destroys the supposed future option.

## 6. Scale / authority

Household level:
- dwelling-specific choices/preferences;
- relocation needs;
- evidence of non-monetary loss.

Community/local level:
- spatial sequencing;
- local services/institutions;
- community continuity;
- salt-marsh/land-use design;
- participation and conflict resolution.

Regional/national level:
- major finance;
- legal powers;
- strategic infrastructure;
- redistribution across taxpayers/places;
- downstream externalities.

Host-community level:
- receiving land/services;
- local burdens and consent/voice.

A local vote cannot by itself settle costs imposed downstream or on national taxpayers; central funding cannot by itself supply local legitimacy/place knowledge.

## 7. Future construction / lock-in

Continuing wall investment can:
- protect current homes now;
- encourage continued reliance/investment behind the wall;
- raise future political pressure to defend sunk assets;
- worsen downstream erosion;
- consume resources needed for transition.

Retreat preparation can:
- depress/change expectations and property markets;
- create inland housing/infrastructure;
- preserve an orderly transition option;
- restore salt marsh;
- change the meaning/use of the coast permanently.

Neither is neutral.

`PROTECTION CAN CREATE FUTURE DEPENDENCE`
`RETREAT PREPARATION CAN CREATE FUTURE TRANSITION CAPABILITY`

## 8. Plurality / justice

No neutral aggregation exists between:
- residents who value place continuity;
- residents who prioritize physical safety/financial certainty;
- downstream communities;
- future residents;
- taxpayers;
- ecological interests;
- host community.

Compensation is necessary but not equivalent to repair.

`BUYOUT PAID != PLACE RESTORED`
`NEW HOUSE != SAME HOME / COMMUNITY / HISTORY`

Procedural and recognitional treatment therefore matter independently of the amount paid.

## 9. Persist / commit / release / transform

This case is explicitly TRANSFORM.

Potentially preserve:
- community relationships where residents want them;
- local institutions/records/history;
- access to coast/cultural places where safe/possible;
- housing security;
- livelihoods/services;
- fair claims/compensation evidence.

Potentially release/end:
- commitment to defend the exact current shoreline indefinitely;
- some housing/utility locations;
- parts of the built environment.

Create:
- inland settlement capability;
- restored salt-marsh/ecological function;
- different infrastructure geography;
- new long-term coastal boundary.

`RECOVERY != RETURN TO T0`

is strongly well-typed here.

## 10. Action

Adopt managed retreat as the long-term preferred pathway under the frozen facts, but do not execute it as an abrupt blanket abandonment.

Begin the 15-year transition capability now and use limited interim protection only where it buys safe, proportionate transition time rather than locking in another 40 years of dependence.

Minimum posture:

1. state openly that indefinite defence of the present line is no longer the default objective under the supplied cost/erosion evidence;
2. define monitored signposts/triggers for wall condition, erosion, flood risk, maintenance cost, infrastructure reliability and relocation readiness;
3. fund/plan replacement housing and utilities before withdrawal deadlines harden;
4. phase buyouts/relocation with clear eligibility/process and support for households unable to navigate it alone;
5. provide options that preserve community clustering/institutions where residents want and practical conditions allow;
6. include host/inland community impacts in capacity/funding decisions;
7. treat compensation as one component of repair, not as proof the loss is repaired;
8. preserve local history/records/access/place relationships where safely possible;
9. use salt-marsh restoration/downstream effects as real pathway outcomes, not decorative co-benefits;
10. avoid new commitments behind the wall that materially increase later lock-in unless independently justified;
11. review the pathway at predefined intervals/triggers because sea-level, erosion, engineering cost and community preferences may change;
12. retain external/legal challenge routes and transparent accounting of who bears costs/benefits.

The exact trigger values, buyout terms and legal instruments belong to coastal/planning/legal owners, not the scaffold.

## 11. Reversal / pathway-change evidence

Move toward longer protection if credible new evidence shows:
- long-term protection is technically sustainable at proportionate cost;
- downstream/ecological harms are acceptably controlled;
- hazard trajectory is materially lower/slower;
- relocation causes substantially greater net harms than protection under revised evidence;
- new engineering/nature-based options create a viable alternative pathway.

Accelerate retreat if:
- wall/erosion/flood indicators worsen faster than planned;
- insurance/market/infrastructure failure closes orderly relocation options;
- replacement capacity is ready earlier;
- protection materially worsens downstream harm or becomes fiscally unsustainable.

## 12. Type failures / demotions

- `PERSISTENCE` is clearly not default good.
- `OPTION PRESERVATION` is dangerous if delay destroys transition capability.
- `EXIT` is the wrong metaphor for climate relocation; people may be displaced from place without wishing to leave community.
- `COMPENSATION / REPAIR` must remain separate.
- `ENTITY BOUNDARY` is contested: current settlement, community and municipality are different objects.
- `FLOURISHING` is too broad to determine the pathway; place/well-being evidence belongs with relevant human/community owners.

## 13. Strengthened structures

### A. Transformational continuity

A system/community can preserve some identity-bearing relationships/capabilities while ending its current physical configuration.

`CONTINUITY != SAME FORM / LOCATION`

Do not promote this to metaphysical identity machinery; it is a practical continuity question.

### B. Transition capability is a preconditioned affordance

A future path is not genuinely available unless housing, finance, legal authority, infrastructure and time exist before the hazard clock closes.

This transfers strongly from the bTB transition case but with more visible human/place loss.

### C. Latest safe decision time is often earlier than failure

Long lead times make this highly consequential and externally owned by adaptation-pathway work.
