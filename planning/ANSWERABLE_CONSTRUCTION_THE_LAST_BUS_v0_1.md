# The last bus

*A construction narrative for the Answerable Construction reader. Working draft, 11 September 2026.*

This is an invented situation, not a report about a college or a transport provider. Its arrangements are proposals for reasoning, not tested services, legal requirements or instructions to operators. It extends the reader's evening-course example without adding a general decision rule.

## A place on the course

The college has offered an evening course. The tutor is available, the room is booked and there is no fee. People who could not attend during working hours finally have somewhere to go.

Except that the last bus leaves before the lesson ends.

One learner can drive. Another can sometimes ask a neighbour for a lift. A third cannot pay for a taxi and has nobody to call. The course is open to all three in the prospectus. It is not equally available in their lives.

There is something worth building here, not merely something to prevent. A reliable way home could let the third learner attend. A regular group could make sustained teaching possible. What begins as a transport difficulty is also a question about whether this new shared activity can exist.

The college cannot make it exist by declaring transport somebody else's responsibility. Nor can it order a bus company to run a service simply because that would make the course work. The room, teaching time, transport and learners' own commitments belong to different people. A workable proposal has to connect them without pretending one party owns all the decisions.

## The offer behind the invitation

Suppose the college considers funding a small return service for the course's first term. This is one possible arrangement, not necessarily the best one. Moving the class earlier could exclude shift workers. Remote attendance could help some learners and fail others who lack suitable space, access or equipment. Choosing transport would spend money that could support a different course or service.

The college's authorised budget holder would have to commit actual funding. The transport provider would have to accept a service it can deliver. Staff would need to know who handles a missed departure. Prospective learners could challenge the timing and proposed stops without being made responsible for designing the entire service themselves.

Their contributions are not interchangeable. A learner knows something important about the journey they can make. That does not give them access to the college's budget. A budget holder can authorise spending but cannot know every learner's circumstances from an attendance forecast. The provider knows its operational constraints but does not thereby decide what education should be offered.

The useful construction is the connection among these commitments. A signed transport arrangement without money to pay for it is not the same offer. Money without a provider is not a journey. A journey that leaves before the class ends recreates the original problem under a different name.

Only after the relevant parties have made commitments they can actually carry should the college describe this particular return service as available. Unresolved details need not stop every kind of preparation. The tutor can prepare material while transport is being arranged. But preparation cannot be advertised as a completed route home.

## The evening it does not arrive

Now suppose the vehicle fails to appear after a class.

The learner has done everything the invitation asked. They enrolled, attended and arrived at the agreed stop. It is a mistake to make them become the coordinator of the failed arrangement simply because they are the person standing there.

A proposed fallback would give a named college contact access to a funded alternative, with the ability to book it directly where practicable. The learner would not have to advance money they do not have and later establish a claim. Information about that contact would have to be available in a usable form, rather than only through an account or device the learner might not have.

This is still a proposal with dependencies. An emergency contact who has finished work cannot respond. An alternative provider may have no vehicle available. A nominal reserve may already have been spent. Writing the fallback into a document establishes none of those conditions.

If no safe alternative can be provided that evening, the college must not describe the support as delivered. A staff member may be able to help within their actual role and resources; they cannot promise capacity they lack. The failed journey remains a failure even if everybody acted conscientiously once they learned about it.

The cost is not confined to transport. The learner may miss a caring commitment, lose trust in the course or decide they cannot risk attending again. A later apology or refund cannot simply put the evening back. Recording those effects helps keep them in subsequent decisions, but does not itself repair them.

## When the first term ends

Suppose the service works often enough to establish a regular class, but the first-term funding is ending. Continuing it would require money currently intended for something else.

The group has now formed around an arrangement. People have adjusted shifts, organised care or turned down another opportunity. Their reliance matters. It does not prove that the transport subsidy must continue forever, regardless of every competing need.

Equally, the college cannot erase that reliance by redefining the course as an online offering and counting everyone as still served. A changed offer has different requirements. Some learners might welcome it. Others might lose the opportunity the original commitment created.

A more answerable transition would make the funding limit and proposed change visible while alternatives remain possible. The college could explore a changed timetable, a different transport arrangement or a combination of in-person and remote participation. The people affected could say which of those routes they could actually use. The proposal would still have to name who can authorise and fund it.

None of this supplies a neutral answer to the funding conflict. Another group may need the same money just as urgently. Giving every current participant influence does not represent those excluded from the room. Continuing the existing course has a cost; ending it has a cost. A careful record can expose both without proving which should take priority.

## What was built

The positive achievement, if the arrangement really operated, would be more than a bus contract. It would be a course people could attend and a group able to continue learning together. The materials of that achievement would include teaching, money, a journey, reliable information and the work of responding when things went wrong.

That is also why one successful first night would not settle the arrangement's durability. The reserve could run out. The provider could withdraw. Learners' working hours could change. The course might itself prove unhelpful. Creating a route is not proof that its destination is worthwhile for everyone who uses it.

The example proposes three concrete changes to the initial offer: connect enrolment to a funded return service; make a failed journey an organisational response obligation rather than a reimbursement burden on a stranded learner; and treat ending the support as a change to the opportunity, not merely an internal budget adjustment.

Each proposal could be infeasible or inferior to a different arrangement. The choices belong with actual participants, providers and authorised institutions, using the evidence and responsibilities of their setting. No new project terminology is needed if ordinary practice already makes the connections clear.

The narrower point is that a shared future is made from commitments people can rely on, including what happens when those commitments fail. Counting available places on a course would miss that work. Counting completed forms about access would miss it too.

## Basis and boundary

This narrative develops the invented evening-course example in [the reader](ANSWERABLE_CONSTRUCTION_READER_v0_1.md) and the existing casebook's distinctions between promised provision, usable access, support, authority and residual loss. It is a design elaboration, not additional evidence for the framework. No institution, learner, transport service, cost or result has been observed here.

Open questions remain: which uses of the budget take priority, what support can actually be delivered, what responsibility survives a failed promise, and whose interests are absent from the decision. The narrative provides no clinical, legal or universal moral determination. It is not a worksheet, a PSFH publication or a revision to TRACE or Mechanical Ethics.
