# Worked Positive Construction — The Woodland Commons

Status: **CONSTRUCTED SCENE / OWNER-FIRST LEARNING AID / NOT POLICY / NOT EVIDENCE / NOT A TEST / NOT A UNIVERSAL COMMONS MODEL**

This scene is rebuilt from one useful case buried inside the retired 1 September transfer harness.

The test harness remains retired. Nothing here reopens TRACE or Mechanical Ethics testing.

The scene is kept for a different reason:

> it shows a kind of positive construction the Heat Hub does not — people who already share a resource trying to build the rules, contributions, reserves and institutions that let the shared good remain usable over time.

Mature commons, cooperative-governance, property, association and institutional-design traditions own much of this territory. This project does not claim their mechanisms as discoveries.

## Scene

Forty households jointly own a small woodland and a community hall attached to their homes.

The arrangement has existed for decades. Residents can walk in the woodland, book the hall, hold events and use some shared equipment.

The legal co-ownership still exists, but the practical commons is deteriorating.

- five volunteers do most maintenance and administration;
- the hall roof will need major work within several years;
- some households use the hall frequently but contribute little time or money;
- some older and disabled residents cannot contribute physical labour easily;
- several lower-income households say a large annual charge would make the commons feel less like something they co-own;
- booking conflicts are increasingly settled through whoever reaches the volunteer coordinator first;
- minor damage is often absorbed by the same small volunteer group;
- there is no meaningful reserve for major repair.

No single dramatic failure has occurred.

The shared capability is eroding through ordinary use, unequal contribution and deferred maintenance.

```text
LEGAL_COOWNERSHIP != DURABLE_COMMONS_CAPABILITY
RESOURCE_STILL_EXISTS != SHARED_GOOD_REMAINS_USABLE
```

## The proposed construction

Residents consider replacing the informal arrangement with a small governed commons structure:

- modest annual dues;
- reduced or waived dues where needed;
- contribution alternatives such as administration, gardening, maintenance, event support or other useful work where appropriate;
- elected stewards with fixed terms;
- published accounts and maintenance plans;
- transparent booking rules;
- a reserve fund for major repairs;
- simple conflict and appeal routes;
- graduated responses to repeated damage or persistent breach rather than immediate exclusion;
- periodic review of the rules.

The proposal removes some individual freedom.

It also aims to create something that the current informal arrangement increasingly cannot provide:

> a shared place that remains maintained, accessible, financially viable and governable without depending indefinitely on a small number of exhausted volunteers.

That is a constructive claim, not a conclusion that this particular proposal is legitimate.

## 1. Shared goods can require contribution

A hall, woodland or tool store can be jointly owned while the work that keeps it usable is privately borne.

If enough people benefit while maintenance is optional, the resource can degrade even though no individual act looks decisive.

The practical object is therefore not only access to an existing asset.

It is the continuing capability to maintain the shared good.

```text
RIGHT_TO_USE != CAPABILITY_TO_SUSTAIN
INDIVIDUAL_USE_CAN_BE_REASONABLE
AND
AGGREGATE_PATTERN_CAN_DEGRADE_THE_COMMONS
```

Calling this a free-rider problem may sometimes be analytically useful, but the label must not erase differences between unwillingness and inability to contribute.

A resident with little money, limited mobility or heavy caring responsibilities is not made equivalent to someone who can contribute and persistently chooses not to simply because both supplied fewer volunteer hours.

```text
LOW_CONTRIBUTION != SAME_REASON
EQUAL_RULE != EQUAL_BURDEN
```

## 2. Collective constraint can create substantive freedom

One resident objects:

> I already co-own this place. Why should a committee tell me when I can use it or charge me to maintain something that is already mine?

That objection is real.

Booking rules, dues and maintenance obligations constrain what a household can do immediately.

But the absence of shared constraints can also produce a different loss of freedom:

- the roof fails and the hall closes;
- bookings become dependent on personal relationships with a volunteer;
- the five active volunteers burn out and stop administering access;
- households willing to contribute cannot create a repair reserve because others can rely on the asset without any durable contribution mechanism.

So:

```text
LESS_RULE != MORE_SUBSTANTIVE_FREEDOM_BY_DEFAULT
CONSTRAINT_CAN_HELP_CREATE_SHARED_CAPABILITY
```

This does not settle which rules are justified. It makes visible that no-rule and rule are both institutional choices with consequences.

## 3. Authority has to be constructed too

The proposal creates elected stewards.

That gives the group a practical decision route, but an election does not turn every steward decision into legitimate authority by definition.

A bounded steward role might include:

- executing an agreed maintenance budget;
- arranging routine repairs;
- administering bookings under published rules;
- proposing changes to dues or reserve targets;
- responding to damage under an agreed graduated process;
- publishing accounts and conflicts of interest.

It need not include unilateral power to:

- sell the woodland;
- permanently exclude a co-owner;
- change ownership shares;
- spend the reserve on unrelated projects;
- rewrite contribution rules without the agreed collective process.

```text
DELEGATED_STEWARDSHIP != SOVEREIGNTY
ELECTION != UNBOUNDED_AUTHORITY
COMPETENCE != LEGITIMACY
```

The point of delegation is not to eliminate disagreement. It is to make some necessary collective actions possible without requiring forty households to decide every minor repair.

## 4. Contribution needs more than one route

If participation is reduced to a flat cash fee, the commons may become easier for wealthier households and harder for lower-income co-owners.

If participation is reduced to physical work, the arrangement can burden older, disabled, time-poor or caring households unfairly.

A stronger design can distinguish contribution from one mandatory medium.

Possible routes might include:

- money;
- practical maintenance;
- administration;
- governance work;
- event support;
- other agreed contributions;
- reduced/waived contribution where ability is genuinely constrained.

None is automatically equivalent.

The group still has to decide what contribution is adequate and how to prevent the flexibility itself becoming opaque favouritism.

```text
SHARED_OBLIGATION != ONE_IDENTICAL_PAYMENT_ROUTE
FLEXIBILITY != NO_RULE
```

The more capable or better-resourced members may reasonably carry more complexity, but that does not create a universal rule that every burden must be equalised.

## 5. Sanctions can protect a commons without becoming punishment as identity

Suppose repeated careless use damages shared equipment.

A system with no response can export the cost to everyone else and to the volunteers who repair it.

A system that jumps immediately to permanent exclusion can convert a maintenance problem into loss of belonging and property access.

The proposed structure therefore uses graduated responses, for example:

```text
NOTICE / EXPLANATION
-> REQUESTED REPAIR OR CONTRIBUTION WHERE APPROPRIATE
-> TEMPORARY USE CONDITION
-> STRONGER COLLECTIVE REVIEW FOR REPEATED / SERIOUS BREACH
```

The exact mechanism belongs to the relevant legal and commons-governance owners.

The portable distinction is smaller:

```text
ENFORCEMENT != DOMINATION_BY_DEFINITION
NO_ENFORCEMENT != FAIRNESS_BY_DEFAULT
SANCTIONED_ACT != PERSON_REDEFINED_AS_BAD_MEMBER
```

Challenge and review matter because steward power can also be mistaken, selective or captured.

## 6. A reserve is a commitment to future co-owners

The roof does not fail every year.

A reserve fund can therefore look like money being taken from current households for no current service.

But the future repair obligation is already being created by continued use and ageing infrastructure.

A reserve converts part of that future burden into present capacity.

```text
NO_CURRENT_FAILURE != NO_FUTURE_OBLIGATION
RESERVE_NOW -> FUTURE_REPAIR_CAPABILITY
```

This also creates an intertemporal governance problem.

Current members should not be able to spend the reserve casually merely because the future members who will need the roof cannot vote today.

Nor should a reserve target become untouchable merely because an earlier generation chose it.

The target and its purpose remain reviewable as costs, membership and the asset change.

## 7. Exit is not the whole answer

A household can in principle sell its home and leave the commons.

That formal exit matters, but it is not a complete answer to bad governance.

The woodland and hall may be tied to:

- home;
- neighbourhood relationships;
- children's friendships;
- local history;
- ordinary routines;
- property value;
- a resident's sense of belonging.

Telling a dissenting co-owner to “leave if you do not like the rules” can therefore make formal exit carry burdens far beyond the disputed booking or dues rule.

```text
FORMAL_EXIT_EXISTS != GOVERNANCE_IS_LEGITIMATE
PRACTICAL_EXIT_COST != ZERO
CONTINUED_MEMBERSHIP != CONSENT_TO_EVERY_RULE
```

This is why contest, revision and bounded authority matter inside durable shared projects.

## 8. Transparency is useful only if it supports answer-back

Published accounts are not good merely because numbers are visible.

They become part of shared capability when residents can use them to ask questions such as:

- Why did maintenance cost twice the estimate?
- Why was this contractor chosen?
- Is the reserve actually ring-fenced for the purpose residents were told?
- Are booking exceptions being applied consistently?
- Is one steward allocating work to a connected supplier?

```text
PUBLICATION != ANSWERABILITY_BY_ITSELF
VISIBLE_RECORD + USABLE_CHALLENGE_ROUTE
CAN
-> BETTER_COLLECTIVE_CORRECTION
```

Perfect participation is not required for every minor choice. But material commitments need enough legibility that authority does not disappear into administration.

## 9. Use should change the rules

After the new arrangement operates for a year, several things become visible.

- the original booking window disadvantages shift workers;
- work-credit recording creates more clerical burden than expected;
- one maintenance weekend model excludes some households but a smaller recurring rota works better;
- the reserve contribution is adequate for the roof forecast but not for an unexpected drainage problem;
- some residents value woodland access far more than hall use and question whether costs are being allocated sensibly.

These are not automatically proof that the arrangement succeeded or failed.

They are evidence about the constructed institution.

A living commons can revise:

- contribution routes;
- booking rules;
- steward procedures;
- reserve targets;
- maintenance methods;
- conflict routes.

But revision should not erase earlier burdens or rewrite prior unpopular decisions as though they never occurred.

```text
RULE_CHANGE != HISTORY_ERASED
ADAPTATION != LEGITIMACY_PROOF
```

## 10. The commons is allowed to end or transform

Positive construction does not mean every shared institution deserves indefinite preservation.

The hall might eventually become uneconomic to maintain. The woodland might need a different ecological management regime. Membership or surrounding law may change.

The group may eventually decide to transform, merge or dissolve the arrangement.

That decision can itself create losses and obligations.

```text
BUILT_SHARED_GOOD != PERMANENT_ENTITLEMENT_TO_SURVIVE
PRESERVING_INSTITUTION != PRESERVING_ITS_PURPOSE_BY_DEFAULT
```

The constructive question remains what capability, relationship or obligation should persist even if the present organisational form should not.

## What has actually been constructed in the scene

The proposed object is not merely a committee.

It is a coupled shared capability:

```text
COMMON RESOURCE
+ CONTRIBUTION ROUTES
+ MAINTENANCE WORK
+ DELEGATED STEWARDSHIP
+ ACCOUNTABLE RULES
+ RESERVE CAPACITY
+ CONFLICT / CHALLENGE ROUTES
+ ADAPTATION
```

If those parts function together, households gain a future possibility none can reliably create alone:

> a woodland and hall that remain usable across time without depending on a few people carrying invisible work indefinitely.

## Owner boundary

This scene borrows heavily from mature commons, cooperative, property, institutional-governance and collective-action traditions.

It does not establish a local theory of commons governance and does not turn any neighbouring owner's design principles into project canon.

Use those owners where real institutional design, property rights, sanctions, fiduciary duties or legal authority matter.

```text
OWNER_PRACTICE != PROJECT_DISCOVERY
SHARED_ROUTE != PROJECT_OWNERSHIP
```

## What this scene does not establish

It does not establish:

- that annual dues are the right mechanism for a real commons;
- that elections make collective authority legitimate;
- that work credits are always fair;
- that graduated sanctions fit every legal/property arrangement;
- that community consent substitutes for individual rights or wider affected scope;
- that every co-owned resource should be preserved;
- that people unable to contribute owe labour or disclosure of private circumstances;
- that the woodland's ecological interests are exhausted by the human co-owners' preferences;
- that TRACE or Mechanical Ethics has been validated.

It is a worked construction about **creating and sustaining a shared good**, not a proof object.
