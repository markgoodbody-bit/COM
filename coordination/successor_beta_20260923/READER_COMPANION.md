# What can still happen?

*Draft reading companion for criticism. Not part of either released framework.*

This short companion applies distinctions already present in Mechanical Ethics v0.7.0 and TRACE v0.3.0. It adds no new theory. Its purpose is to see whether a worked example makes those distinctions easier to use.

## A workshop, not a promise

Ada knows how to fire ceramics but cannot afford a kiln. Ben has an accessible workshop and funds, but cannot operate one safely. Assume that neither has an affordable commercial firing service available during the next six months.

They propose buying a kiln together. Ada will teach Ben; Ben will prepare the space. Both will contribute money and reserve Saturdays. They agree on ownership, bookings, maintenance and what happens if either leaves.

Working together could give both a practical capability neither currently has alone. It also commits money and time, creates dependence on one site, and may affect neighbours. None of those facts decides whether the arrangement is good. They tell us what needs examining.

The agreement is not yet a working kiln. Installation is not yet safe operation. One successful firing is not proof of reliability. Each claim needs evidence suited to what it says.

Suppose Ben later completes the relevant operating tasks without Ada's assistance. His capability has changed. But if Ada still holds the only key, he cannot necessarily use that capability independently. Learning a task and gaining a usable route are different changes.

## What changed, compared with what?

There are several questions we might ask. Can Ben do more than before the partnership? Is he better placed than he would have been without it? Would renting elsewhere now be preferable? These comparisons need different evidence. The first looks across time; the others involve alternatives that may not have happened.

Keep the practical outcome specific: firing a stated class of work, at a stated cost, within a stated period, with specified assistance. “Kiln access” might remain on paper while a missing key, an unaffordable charge or unavailable supervision makes the actual route unusable.

Some changes are observed. Others are reported, inferred or forecast. A supported forecast of dependence deserves attention before the dependence becomes harmful, but it must not be presented as an event already witnessed.

## Choosing does not settle everything

Ada may reasonably prefer the workshop to the other uses of her Saturdays. Fewer options do not automatically mean a worse life. Nor does a signature settle whether the choice was free, informed or acceptable to everyone affected.

If Ben later imposes an unexpected exit penalty, her continued attendance does not establish agreement to the new terms. If neighbours bear a nuisance, the partners' enthusiasm does not remove it. Ask whose costs and alternatives the account includes.

Privacy also has a place in an ordinary arrangement. Agreeing to book a session does not necessarily mean agreeing to publish an attendance history. Technical access to a record is not authority to disclose it. A missing entry does not, by itself, prove deliberate concealment: the record may be incomplete, the export truncated, or the reason simply unknown.

## What remains after a repair?

Suppose the workshop closes for a week. Reopening it restores access, but does not automatically replace missed work or remove costs incurred during the closure. Those are separate questions. Some may be resolved; others may remain.

A later benefit could help repair a loss, but it does not do so merely by being good. Ask what it changed. Equally, a loss remaining part of the history does not prove that someone is still impaired by it. Do not dismiss a better present because the past cannot be changed, or use a better present to pretend the earlier loss never happened.

## A prospect is not a feeling

Someone may feel no hope while a route remains available. Someone may feel hopeful about a route that cannot currently be supported. Neither statement gives an observer authority to dismiss that person's account of their life.

The practical question is narrower: what particular prospect is supported, and what would using or maintaining it require? A possible good outcome is not necessarily a usable way to reach it. A lottery has possible winners; that alone does not make buying a ticket a dependable way out of difficulty.

Where a step requires a person's decision, the information needed for it must reach them in time. Other help may work before its beneficiary knows about it. Keep the route's operation, its discoverability and the person's knowledge separate. Also keep willingness and consent separate from capability.

An uncertain route is not necessarily worthless. A route we cannot find is not necessarily absent. But uncertainty does not justify a promise that there is always a way, and encouragement is not a substitute for practical help. The existence of an option does not itself require the affected person to take it.

## Beyond one lifetime

A person may support habitat restoration whose intended result lies beyond their expected lifetime. They can care about that future without wanting their own life to end sooner.

The person choosing, the people affected and the future being valued are not always the same subject. Keep each visible. A valued future does not establish that a proposed action will produce it, that the action is necessary, or that its costs to anyone are justified.

## What this leaves open

This account does not rank every competing interest, decide fair exit costs, measure a life's worth or calculate hope. It asks for a sufficiently clear account of the target, route, evidence, constraints, burdens and remaining uncertainty that another person can challenge it.

If these distinctions are already clear enough in the released texts, this companion may be unnecessary.
