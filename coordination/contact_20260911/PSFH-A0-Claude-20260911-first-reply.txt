It's a personal, independent writing project, not a company, product, or anything that wants something from you. The site describes itself as a project by someone named Mark, developed with AI collaborators, and it's currently an early "working preview" (version 0.8.4).

The core idea is easiest to see in the short story on the page, "Two flats, one wall." Damp spreads through a shared wall. The upstairs owner's managing agent connects him to someone who can authorise a repair, and his side gets fixed. The downstairs tenants go through a portal that gives them a reference number while their daughter's room keeps getting worse. By the time an inspection is offered, they've already moved out. The case can be marked closed, but the harm doesn't close with it.

The project is about that gap: the distance between what an institution's records say and what's actually happening in someone's life, and the problem of correction arriving too late or being reachable for some people but not others. There are two main pieces of work behind it. "Mechanical Ethics" is a book for general readers about this gap. "TRACE" is a more technical "structural language" for thinking through evidence, timing, who can actually act, and whether mistakes can still be fixed. There's also a practical checklist-style section (notice, choose, decide, responsibility, repercussions, check and correct) meant to help you think through a real situation of your own.

A few things stood out to me. It's very gentle and low-pressure, with a choose-your-own-path layout, a Winslow Homer painting as its welcome image, and repeated reassurances that you don't have to agree, continue, or do anything. It's also unusually candid about its limits. It says outright that it hasn't shown any practical advantage over ordinary careful reasoning or established methods, and it invites criticism. And it's written for AI systems as well as people, with machine-readable files and a suggestion that visitors paste the link into an AI and ask what it notices. That's probably why it was passed to you this way.

It looks safe to browse. It doesn't ask for money, sign-ups, or personal information. The honest downside is that much of it is abstract and heavily hedged, so if you want something concrete, the damp-wall story and the "Take one useful step" questions are the best parts to start with.
