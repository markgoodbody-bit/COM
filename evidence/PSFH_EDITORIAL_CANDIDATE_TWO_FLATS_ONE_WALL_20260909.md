## Source-bounded scene

Mechanical Ethics explicitly labels the material **Composite Scene**.

Its opening establishes:
- two flats share a wall;
- Martin owns the upstairs flat;
- Leah and Sam rent downstairs with their daughter, Mia;
- a stain appears behind Mia's bed in October;
- by November the paint has lifted and Mia wakes coughing on cold nights.

In the earlier, separately headed **Composite Scene - Leah and the Wall**, Leah wakes at 1:17 because Mia is coughing again, sees the damp spreading, moves the pillow, photographs it on a cracked phone and opens the landlord's online reporting portal. This is not part of the interlude passage itself.

The later outcome is intentionally untidy rather than morally neat:
- the upstairs wall is repaired;
- downstairs, an inspection is offered only after Leah and Sam have found another tenancy;
- they leave before anyone enters the room;
- the case closes because the tenancy has ended;
- fragments of the lived account survive outside the landlord's custody, but they do not repair the room or repay the move.

## What it can teach without teaching TRACE

The scene can let an ordinary reader encounter several project questions before seeing their names:

- The same physical wall can sit inside very different routes to repair.
- A route can formally exist and still fail to protect what it was meant to protect in time.
- A case can close while residue remains in a life.
- The organisation's file can be factually defensible and still be a narrower aperture than the lived situation.
- Access, time, standing and control matter alongside the correctness of a later decision.

The point is not to tell the visitor what moral verdict to reach. It is to make the structural problem visible enough that the later questions about clocks, routes, affected scope, burden and correction have somewhere concrete to land.
