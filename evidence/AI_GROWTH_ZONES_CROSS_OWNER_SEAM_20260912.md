# AI Growth Zones — grid, water, commitment and local benefit

Status: **FIELD NOTE / OWNER-FIRST CROSS-OWNER SEAM / NOT CANON / NOT TRACE OR ME CHANGE / NOT POLICY ENDORSEMENT / NOT CONSULTATION RESPONSE**  
Date checked: **12 September 2026**  
Country: United Kingdom

> **HOW CAN WE MAKE A BETTER FUTURE?**

This note records a live construction problem rather than a failure case.

The UK is trying to build substantially more domestic AI-compute capacity through AI Growth Zones (AIGZs). The useful project question is not whether that policy is simply good or bad. It is whether the relationships needed to make the capability real remain visible as the object passes between national strategy, grid allocation, developer commitment, finance, water, planning, local benefit and long-lived infrastructure.

This is exactly the kind of seam where every specialist owner can be locally competent while consequential relationships still disappear at the handoff.

## Owner facts — current as checked

### 1. AI Growth Zones are intended to accelerate large-scale AI infrastructure

The Department for Science, Innovation and Technology's *Delivering AI Growth Zones* policy paper says AIGZs are intended to unlock large-scale compute by addressing major barriers including electricity-grid access and planning delay. It also says zones should deliver for local people through workforce development, adoption and investment anchored in local economies.

Source:
- DSIT, *Delivering AI Growth Zones*, 13 November 2025: https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/delivering-ai-growth-zones/delivering-ai-growth-zones

### 2. The applicant threshold is already an infrastructure-scale commitment

Current AIGZ application guidance requires a site to demonstrate access to at least **500 MW of power capacity by 2030**, either through secured/formally allocated network capacity or a credible behind-the-meter solution. The same guidance requires sufficient **water availability and discharge** to support at least 500 MW of AI infrastructure.

The criteria also include land, planning, connectivity, delivery feasibility, local impact and the level of government support requested.

Source:
- DSIT, *AI Growth Zones: open for applications*, updated 13 June 2025: https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ai-growth-zones/ai-growth-zones-open-for-applications

### 3. Grid capacity is being treated as a strategic allocation problem, not only a first-come queue

Government's AIGZ delivery policy says it intends to manage speculative data-centre demand, reallocate released capacity and reserve future capacity for AIGZs and other strategically important projects. It also proposes support for developers to build high-voltage grid infrastructure and a targeted electricity-pricing support mechanism in specified regions where additional demand is expected to create measurable system benefits.

Separately, the Department for Energy Security and Net Zero consulted in March 2026 on mechanisms to address speculative demand and prioritise future network capacity for strategic demand including data centres.

Sources:
- DSIT, *Delivering AI Growth Zones*: https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/delivering-ai-growth-zones/delivering-ai-growth-zones
- DESNZ, *Accelerating electricity network connections for strategic demand*, March 2026: https://www.gov.uk/government/consultations/accelerating-electricity-network-connections-for-strategic-demand/accelerating-electricity-network-connections-for-strategic-demand-accessible-webpage

### 4. Ofgem is now trying to attach costly commitment evidence to data-centre queue positions

Ofgem's live consultation published **29 July 2026** and closing **16 September 2026** proposes two data-centre-specific measures:

- a commitment fee held while a project progresses toward connection, with loss of the fee if the project does not progress under the rules;
- queue-management milestones requiring evidence including a credible end user, procurement of long-lead electrical equipment, and financial/technical capability.

Ofgem describes the aim as prioritising viable projects and reducing the negative impact of non-viable projects in the queue.

Its accompanying release says demand connection applications rose from **41 GW to 125 GW in under a year**, driven largely by data-centre projects accounting for at least **80 GW**.

Sources:
- Ofgem, *Proposed data centre connection reforms*, 29 July 2026: https://www.ofgem.gov.uk/consultation/proposed-data-centre-connection-reforms
- Ofgem, *Ofgem acts to free up grid capacity by tackling speculative data centre projects*, 29 July 2026: https://www.ofgem.gov.uk/press-release/ofgem-acts-free-grid-capacity-tackling-speculative-data-centre-projects

### 5. Demand connections reform has multiple simultaneous objectives

Ofgem's June 2026 update describes three problems: a large queue containing likely non-viable projects; well-progressed projects unable to connect quickly because of network/generation build and queue blockers; and the absence of mechanisms to prioritise strategically important demand.

Ofgem says the reform objective is to enable viable and strategic projects to connect while maintaining system security and operational integrity. Its Connect work includes possible self-build/ownership or self-build/transfer of high-voltage assets and operating arrangements intended to reduce negative impacts of large demand or enable positive grid effects.

Source:
- Ofgem, *Connect update: demand connections reform*, 16 June 2026: https://www.ofgem.gov.uk/policy/connect-update-demand-connections-reform

## Cross-owner seam

The same AIGZ object is simultaneously:

- a national AI-capability project;
- a very large electricity-demand project;
- a grid-capacity allocation decision;
- a planning and land-use project;
- a water-availability and discharge project;
- a private-capital and commitment problem;
- a local employment/infrastructure proposition;
- a long-lived physical dependency;
- potentially a public-support / pricing-support object;
- part of wider decarbonisation, resilience and industrial policy.

No single owner naturally carries all of those relations.

```text
NATIONAL_STRATEGIC_PRIORITY
!=
GRID_SYSTEM_PRIORITY
!=
LOCAL_PRIORITY
!=
DEVELOPER_FINANCEABILITY
!=
WATER_VIABILITY
```

They can align. They do not align automatically.

## 1. Credible commitment is becoming part of access to scarce shared capacity

The Ofgem proposal is structurally important because it makes a queue position more expensive to hold without evidence of progress.

That is a live instance of an Open Horizon distinction already observed elsewhere:

```text
OPTION_VALUE_TO_ONE_ACTOR
!=
OPTION_COST_TO_THE_SYSTEM
```

A speculative connection reservation can preserve one developer's option while consuming scarce planning/network attention or delaying another project.

The proposed commitment fee and milestones are owner-native mechanisms for asking a project to make its claim more costly/verifiable as that claim becomes more consequential to others.

This does **not** establish that Ofgem's proposed thresholds or fee design are correct. It makes the relation visible.

## 2. Strategic prioritisation creates an answerability question at the point of reallocation

Government is explicitly moving away from treating grid demand purely as an undifferentiated queue.

That can be rational where some projects produce larger system or national benefits. But once strategic priority can reserve or reallocate scarce future capacity, the relevant object is larger than whether the preferred project is itself viable.

Questions include:

- Which alternative uses are delayed or displaced?
- What evidence establishes the claimed strategic benefit?
- How does the priority change when project facts change?
- Which promises to local places or other sectors are coupled to the priority?
- Is there a reassessment route before long-lived infrastructure hardens around the designation?

```text
PROJECT_VIABLE
!=
PROJECT_SHOULD_RECEIVE_PRIORITY_OVER_ALTERNATIVES
```

That second judgement belongs to legitimate policy/governance owners, not to this project.

## 3. Water is a co-equal physical dependency, not a footnote to the power connection

The application criteria explicitly require water availability and discharge at the same 500 MW scale as the power requirement.

That matters because a compute project can be electrically connectable while remaining locally constrained by water, discharge, drought resilience, competing users or infrastructure build.

The useful seam question is:

> when grid, planning and AI-policy processes accelerate, does water remain coupled to the same project clocks and scale assumptions, or is it rediscovered later by another owner?

```text
POWER_ROUTE_USABLE != WHOLE_PROJECT_VIABLE
```

This note does not infer a current water conflict at any named AIGZ. The issue is that water is already a stated load-bearing dependency and therefore should travel with the project object.

## 4. Local benefit must survive translation from policy promise to durable mechanism

Government policy says AIGZs should deliver for local people.

That is a goal statement, not evidence that a particular zone will do so.

A cross-owner reading asks how the stated local benefit becomes durable through project development and ownership change:

- jobs: what kinds, for whom, over what project phases?
- skills: does local capability remain after construction?
- energy/network infrastructure: who pays, owns and benefits from it later?
- local adoption: is compute access/use actually coupled to the host region?
- land/water/environmental burden: who carries it and for how long?
- change of developer/owner: which commitments bind successors?

```text
LOCAL_BENEFIT_STATED != LOCAL_CAPABILITY_DELIVERED
```

The point is not to prescribe the benefit package. It is to stop the relationship disappearing between national designation, developer agreement, planning, finance and later operation.

## 5. Self-build can solve a capacity problem while changing the ownership/control topology

Ofgem and government are considering routes by which large demand users may build high-voltage infrastructure themselves or build and transfer it.

That can accelerate capability creation.

It also changes the questions:

- who owns the asset during each phase?
- who controls operation and maintenance?
- what happens if the data-centre project changes or fails?
- which network benefits or risks remain socialised?
- does a project-specific solution create useful shared infrastructure or a stranded/private dependency?

```text
SELF_BUILD_CAPABILITY != SELF_AUTHORIZED_INFRASTRUCTURE_SOVEREIGNTY
```

Existing electricity-network regulation is the owner here. This project should route outward, not invent its own grid-governance answer.

## 6. Public/system support changes the distribution object

The AIGZ policy includes targeted pricing support where additional data-centre demand is expected to reduce grid constraint costs in specified areas.

That is not simply a subsidy story. The intended logic is that location-specific demand can produce system value.

A useful accounting object therefore includes:

```text
PRIVATE_CAPITAL
+ PUBLIC / SYSTEM SUPPORT
+ NETWORK EFFECT
+ LOCAL BURDEN / BENEFIT
+ LONG_TERM_ASSET / DEMAND
```

rather than reducing the project to `private investment -> private return` or `public support -> public ownership`.

The appropriate allocation of return/control is a policy, regulatory and contractual question. The project contribution is only to keep the full contribution/risk stack visible.

## 7. Infrastructure hardens before the AI use case is finished changing

Grid reinforcement, water infrastructure, land, planning consent and data-centre shells are long-lived relative to AI model/product cycles.

That creates a time seam:

```text
AI_CAPABILITY / BUSINESS_MODEL_CLOCK
!=
GRID / WATER / LAND / INFRASTRUCTURE_CLOCK
```

A project can remain technically useful while its original use case changes; or become strategically less important after infrastructure has been committed.

The relevant correction question is therefore not permanent reversibility. It is what can still be reassessed at each stage, what becomes deliberately committed, and which obligations survive change in use or ownership.

## Current project learning

This case reinforces several already-earned project distinctions without promoting any of them into TRACE or Mechanical Ethics:

```text
CAPITAL_ALLOCATION -> FUTURE_ACTION_SPACE
OPTION_VALUE_TO_ONE_ACTOR != OPTION_COST_TO_THE_SYSTEM
PROJECT_VIABLE != PROJECT_SHOULD_RECEIVE_PRIORITY_OVER_ALTERNATIVES
POWER_ROUTE_USABLE != WHOLE_PROJECT_VIABLE
LOCAL_BENEFIT_STATED != LOCAL_CAPABILITY_DELIVERED
ROUTE_EXISTS != ROUTE_USABLE
COMMITMENT_CAN_CREATE_CAPABILITY
COMMITMENT_CAN_ALSO_HARDEN_BURDEN
```

The strongest cross-owner object is:

> **Can a strategic AI-infrastructure commitment remain connected to the grid capacity it consumes, the water and place it depends on, the capital and public support that make it viable, the local capability it promises, the alternatives it displaces, and the reassessment routes that remain before each dependency hardens?**

That is a field question, not a project decision rule.

## What this note does NOT establish

Do not infer:

- AI Growth Zones are good or bad overall;
- data centres should or should not receive strategic grid priority;
- 500 MW is an appropriate minimum scale;
- a commitment fee is the right queue mechanism;
- a named zone currently lacks water;
- public support deserves a particular ownership stake;
- local authorities or developers are failing;
- this project can resolve electricity-network, planning, water, competition, public-law or investment decisions;
